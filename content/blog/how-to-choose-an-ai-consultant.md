Pick the consultant who can prove their work runs and can show you how they check it. A good pitch is cheap. I build AI automations for small businesses and I work alone, so I'm one of the people you might be weighing. This is the filter I'd use if I were on your side of the call.

## Where should I start looking for an AI consultant?

Start with the people who could plausibly do the job, and test them directly. Skip the ranked lists. [One consultant's guide to choosing an AI firm](https://kengavranovic.com/insights/how-to-choose-an-ai-consulting-firm) points out that searching for the best firm gets you a dozen ranked lists that share almost no names, and most of them are written by a firm that put itself at the top. That includes anything I'd write, so weigh it the same way.

Then filter on size. [An AI strategy firm's list of questions to ask before signing](https://dancumberlandlabs.com/blog/8-questions-ask-ai-consultant) makes the point that a setup built for 10,000 employees won't carry over to a 20-person team. If every example a consultant gives you comes from a company far bigger than yours, they are guessing about your business.

## Do I even need an AI consultant?

Often you don't. If you have one or two simple needs, like drafting emails or summarizing meetings, and your team is already comfortable with the tools, [a small-business AI consultancy's service page](https://cloudmotiv.ai/ai-consulting-austin-tx-small-business) says plainly you can probably set it up yourself. Its suggested order is worth stealing: start with the task that costs the most hours each week, check whether software you already pay for can handle it, and only then buy something new.

Use this as a test on the call. Describe your simplest problem and watch what happens. A consultant worth hiring will tell you when you don't need them. One who turns every problem into a custom build is selling builds.

## What should I have written down before the first call?

The result you want and the number that would prove you got it. [The same consultancy](https://cloudmotiv.ai/ai-consulting-austin-tx-small-business) says success should show up as hours saved or dollars cut, not as more tools bought.

"More efficient" is something nobody can check. "Quotes go out the same day" is.

This also protects you from the consultant. If you show up without a number, they will pick one for you, and it will be one they can hit.

## Which questions separate a builder from a talker?

Start by asking how they learn your work before they recommend anything. A consultant who names a tool before asking how your business runs has an answer looking for a problem. The pre-signing question list puts it in a line I agree with: "The tech is easy. The change is hard." ([source](https://dancumberlandlabs.com/blog/8-questions-ask-ai-consultant))

The rest fit in one call:

| Question | A good answer | Walk away if |
|---|---|---|
| What have you built that is still running? | They show you a live system | All they have is slides and demos |
| What do you need to learn about my work first? | They ask how the task runs today and where the data sits | They name a tool before asking anything |
| How do you test it before I rely on it? | A specific bug they caught, and how | "We test everything" |
| What will I own and be able to see? | Accounts, logins and notes in your name | Everything lives in their accounts |
| Who notices when it breaks? | A plain description of the checks | A shrug, or "it won't break" |

The first row is how you tell a good consultant from what [a buyer's guide to AI consultants](https://de-alwis.com/what-does-an-ai-consultant-do) calls a "slide merchant." Ask to see the thing running. If their best work only lives in a presentation, that tells you most of what you need.

## How can I tell if a consultant checks their own work?

Ask for one specific bug they caught before anyone relied on the system. Vague answers tell you nothing. Here's the kind of answer I'd want to hear, from my own work.

When I built a card fee checker, review flagged 6 serious bugs and tests found more. One left fees collected daily out of the total cost. Another counted fees the tool didn't recognize as overpayment. I fixed them, and by the end all 157 tests passed. It still isn't in front of business owners. That waits until I've run it on real statements.

I check what I say about the work too. Before I published [my playbook on picking a first automation](https://www.wexadvisory.com/blog/pick-your-first-automation), I went through every first-hand claim against my decision logs and every source claim against the live page. My draft had said the fee checker had "6 critical bugs." That mixed what review flagged with what testing found, so I rewrote it to say exactly that.

A consultant who audits their own marketing is more likely to audit your system. Ask them when they last corrected something they had already written down.

## What happens after the system is built?

Something will break eventually. What matters is who notices and what they do next.

My own example: a booking link in my cold email setup stopped working because the meeting type behind it was switched off. 542 cold emails I'd sent since May carried that link. I swapped the link where the emails pull it from, redeployed, confirmed zero dead links on the live pages, and scheduled a check on the next real send to make sure the new link was actually in the email.

That last step is what to listen for. A fix only counts once it's confirmed on the real thing. Someone who says "fixed it" and stops there leaves you to find the next problem yourself.

## Is it risky to hire an AI consultant who works alone?

It can be, and you should ask about it directly. The guide I mentioned earlier has a table of consultant types, and what it says to watch for with a freelance specialist is no continuity and no one to call in month six ([source](https://kengavranovic.com/insights/how-to-choose-an-ai-consulting-firm)). It also says to settle before the first call who inside your business will own the work after the consultant leaves.

Both points apply to me, and to every other solo builder. So ask any of us two things: what you will own and be able to see if we disappear, and which checks keep running without us. If the answers are fuzzy, the risk is real, and a small agency might suit you better.

## Should a good AI consultant admit what didn't work?

Yes, and you should ask them to. Plenty of AI work never pays off. The buyer's guide I linked above cites a McKinsey survey in which nearly nine in ten respondents said their organization uses AI, but only 37% could tie any profit impact to it ([source](https://de-alwis.com/what-does-an-ai-consultant-do)). Anyone claiming a perfect record either hasn't done much or isn't telling you.

Here's one of mine. My first 4 blog posts here got 0 search clicks. I looked at why, decided content depth was a weak spot, and moved to a monthly playbook that ends in a free tool. That change is still proving itself, and I'd say so on a call.

The answer you want has three parts: what failed, what they changed, and no pretending the change already worked.

Before your first call, can you name the one task you want gone and the number that would prove it's gone?
